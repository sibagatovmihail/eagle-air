Maintenance

Most HVAC breakdowns are preventable. Clogged filters, dirty coils, and low refrigerant levels silently degrade performance until the system fails entirely — usually on the hottest or coldest day of the year. Our maintenance plans catch these issues early with scheduled inspections, keeping your system efficient and your warranty intact.

How it works

What a Florida AC tune-up actually includes

An EagleAir maintenance visit is a measurement pass over the parts that fail predictably. The outdoor condenser coil is washed down and the indoor evaporator coil inspected and cleaned. The condensate drain line is flushed and treated, because that line is the single most common cause of a summer shutdown in this climate. Refrigerant pressures are compared against the design charge, the run capacitor is measured against its rated microfarads rather than eyeballed, contactor points and electrical connections are inspected for pitting and heat damage, blower and filter airflow are checked, the float switch is tested, and the thermostat is verified against an independent reading.

Plan on roughly sixty to ninety minutes per visit, and on two visits a year in Southwest Florida: one ahead of the cooling season and one in October or November before the first cold front puts the heat strips to work. Two jobs stay with the homeowner between visits, namely changing the filter every 30 to 90 days and keeping grass clippings, mulch and shrubs away from the outdoor unit. The written service record matters as well, because most manufacturers require documented annual professional maintenance for the parts warranty on a compressor to remain valid.

You are overdue if the system now takes noticeably longer to reach the same setpoint than it did last year, if indoor relative humidity has crept above the 45 to 55 percent band while the temperature is fine, if there is a musty smell at startup, if there is standing water anywhere near the air handler, or if the electricity bill has climbed without a change in weather or habits. ENERGY STAR's 78 degree Fahrenheit summer setpoint is a useful control here: if the house can no longer hold it comfortably, the system has drifted rather than the weather.

Questions

Maintenance questions, answered

Common maintenance questions from Sarasota, Manatee and Charlotte county customers.

An EagleAir maintenance visit covers cleaning the outdoor condenser coil and indoor evaporator coil, flushing and treating the condensate drain line, checking refrigerant pressures against the design charge, measuring capacitor capacitance and inspecting contactors and wiring, checking the blower and filter, and calibrating the thermostat. The point is to find the parts that are degrading — a capacitor reading below specification, a drain line starting to slime up — before they strand you in August.

It is worth it when the system runs as much as Florida systems do. Two visits a year cost far less than a single emergency compressor call, and a clean coil and correct refrigerant charge measurably reduce electricity use over a long cooling season. Maintenance also protects the warranty: most manufacturers require documented annual professional service, and a denied warranty claim on a compressor is an expensive way to learn that.

Every 30 to 90 days, and closer to 30 for a one-inch filter in a house with pets or ongoing construction. Because Florida systems run nearly year-round, filters load faster here than in seasonal climates. A blocked filter starves the evaporator coil of airflow, which freezes the coil, cuts cooling to nothing and then melts into a water leak — one of the most common and most avoidable service calls.

Twice a year: once before the cooling season and once in autumn before the heating side is needed. Florida systems accumulate far more running hours than systems in seasonal climates because they cool for most of the calendar year, so coils foul faster, drain lines biofoul faster and capacitors age faster than the manufacturer's northern assumptions allow for. The autumn visit is not padding: the electric auxiliary heat strips and the reversing valve sit unused for most of the year and can only be tested in heating mode, which is why so many no-heat calls all land on the same cold morning.

Three things, and no more. Change the air filter every 30 to 90 days, nearer 30 for a one-inch filter in a home with pets. Keep at least two feet clear around the outdoor condenser and rinse the coil fins gently with a garden hose after switching the power off at the outdoor disconnect. Flush the condensate drain access with a cup of distilled vinegar each month through the cooling season so algae cannot block the line. Everything beyond that belongs to a technician: capacitors hold a dangerous charge after the power is off, and handling refrigerant requires EPA Section 608 certification.
