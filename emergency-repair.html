How it works

How an EagleAir emergency call works, from phone call to fix

An emergency call starts with triage on the phone. EagleAir answers (904) 316-9933 around the clock, and the first questions asked, whether water is running out of the air handler, whether there is a burning smell, whether the breaker is tripping, and whether anyone in the house is elderly, very young or medically vulnerable, decide both the priority of the call and what the technician loads on the truck. On arrival, power is isolated at the disconnect before anything is opened, the fault is diagnosed, and a flat-rate price is given before the repair begins, at any hour of the night.

Most no-cool emergencies come down to a short list of failures that are carried as van stock: a failed run capacitor, a burned contactor, a blown fuse in the outdoor disconnect, a float switch that has cut the system off because the condensate drain blocked, or a frozen evaporator coil sitting behind a clogged filter. Those are same-visit repairs. When the fault needs an ordered part, such as a compressor, a coil or a control board for an older model, the technician stabilises what can be stabilised, thawing a frozen coil and clearing a blocked drain so the house is not taking water damage while the part is sourced, and gives you a timeline alongside the quote.

Call immediately rather than waiting for the morning if the house has no cooling and anyone in it is elderly, an infant, or living with a heart or respiratory condition, because an unconditioned Florida house tracks the outdoor temperature and humidity within hours. Call immediately for water leaking into a ceiling or an air handler closet, for any burning or electrical smell, and for a breaker that trips again after a single reset. What can usually wait until morning: a dead thermostat battery, one room warmer than the rest, or a system that still cools but runs longer than it used to.

Emergency Repair questions, answered

Common emergency repair questions from Sarasota, Manatee and Charlotte county customers.

No cooling in Florida summer heat, no heat during a winter cold front, water actively leaking from the air handler into the house, a burning smell from the unit, and a system repeatedly tripping its breaker all count. Heat is the real risk: indoor temperatures climb quickly in a Florida house without cooling, and that is dangerous for older residents, infants, and anyone with a heart or respiratory condition. EagleAir answers these calls 24/7 on (904) 316-9933.

Turn the system off at the thermostat if water is leaking or you smell burning — that prevents further damage. Check that the breaker has not tripped and that the thermostat has working batteries, since both are common and free fixes. Do not reset a breaker repeatedly if it keeps tripping; that indicates an electrical fault. Keep interior doors open to even out temperature, and close blinds on sun-facing windows.

EagleAir quotes flat-rate pricing before any work begins, at any hour. You are told the price for the repair before the technician starts, so there is no meter running and no surprise line item on the invoice for the time of day.

No. A breaker that trips more than once is reporting an electrical fault, and resetting it repeatedly risks further equipment damage and a fire hazard. The usual causes are a compressor drawing locked-rotor current because it cannot start, a shorted capacitor or contactor, a condenser coil dirty enough that head pressure and current draw climb, a refrigerant charge low enough to overwork the compressor, damaged wiring at the disconnect, or a breaker weakened by age. Leave the breaker off and call EagleAir on (904) 316-9933; the fault is diagnosed and priced flat-rate before any work starts, 24 hours a day.

Switch the system off at the thermostat and set the fan to ON so the coil can thaw, which normally takes one to four hours, and never chip or scrape at the ice. Then check the air filter, because ice almost always means the coil is being starved of airflow by a clogged filter, a blocked return or a failing blower, or that the refrigerant charge is low. Refrigerant is never consumed, so a low charge always means a leak that has to be located rather than topped up. Running a frozen system risks returning liquid refrigerant to the compressor, which is the most expensive component in the system.
