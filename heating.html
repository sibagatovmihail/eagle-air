How it works

What a heating service call involves in Southwest Florida

A heating service call on a Florida heat pump is largely a test of parts that sit idle eleven months of the year. The technician puts the system into heating mode and confirms the reversing valve actually changes over, measures the temperature rise across the air handler, clamps each electric auxiliary heat strip in turn to confirm it draws current, checks the sequencers, contactors and the breaker feeding them, and verifies that the thermostat stages the strips instead of leaving them energised. Refrigerant charge is checked as well, because a heat pump's heating output depends on charge exactly as its cooling output does.

October or November is the right time to book, and the visit usually runs one to two hours. Electric heat strips draw far more current than anything else in the system, so the electrical inspection is not a formality: loose lugs and heat-damaged wiring at the strip block are a common find on a system that has never been checked. EagleAir quotes a flat rate before work begins. The federal minimum for a new split-system heat pump has been 14.3 SEER2 and 7.5 HSPF2 since 1 January 2023, which is the benchmark any replacement quote should be measured against.

You need the call if any of these show up on the first cold morning. Supply air only slightly warmer than the room while the outdoor unit is running. Heat that arrives only when emergency heat is selected, which means the heat pump side has failed and you are paying resistance-strip rates for every degree. A breaker that trips whenever the heat calls. Or a burning smell that does not clear within a few minutes: dust burning off unused heat strips at the start of the season is normal and brief, a persistent smell is not.

Questions

Heating questions, answered

Common heating questions from Sarasota, Manatee and Charlotte county customers.

Do homes in Sarasota really need heating?

Yes, though the season is short. Sarasota sees cold fronts each winter that drop overnight temperatures into the 40s, and most Florida homes use a heat pump — the same outdoor unit that cools in summer, running in reverse — plus electric auxiliary heat strips for the coldest mornings. Because the heat side is used so little, faults often go unnoticed until the first cold night of the year, which is exactly when every HVAC company in the county is booked.

Why is my heat pump blowing cold air?

The most common causes are a reversing valve stuck in cooling mode, a failed auxiliary heat strip or its sequencer, a thermostat set to 'cool' or wired incorrectly after a replacement, and a defrost cycle — which is normal and brief. A heat pump also delivers air cooler than a gas furnace by design, around 90–95°F, which feels cool against skin at 98°F body temperature even while it is heating correctly.

When should heating be checked before winter?

October or November, before the first front. A pre-season check runs the system in heating mode, verifies each auxiliary heat strip draws current, confirms the reversing valve switches, and checks the thermostat changeover — all things that cannot be tested while the system is cooling. Booking before the season avoids the January queue.

What is emergency heat and should I use it?

Emergency heat bypasses the heat pump entirely and warms the house with electric resistance strips alone, so it should be used only when the heat pump itself has failed. A heat pump moves heat from outdoor air into the house and delivers several units of heat for each unit of electricity, while resistance strips convert electricity to heat one for one, which makes emergency heat substantially more expensive to run. Needing emergency heat to feel warm is itself a fault report rather than a setting choice: it points at the compressor, the reversing valve or the defrost control, and EagleAir can diagnose which on a single visit.

How long does a heat pump last in Florida?

Ten to fifteen years is the realistic service life for a heat pump in Southwest Florida. The compressor accumulates far more running hours here than in a seasonal climate, because the same equipment cools the house for most of the year, and salt exposure near the Gulf shortens outdoor coil life further. The heating side sees very few hours by comparison, so failures there tend to surface as a surprise on the first cold front rather than as a gradual decline.
