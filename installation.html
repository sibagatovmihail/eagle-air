How it works

What a professional HVAC installation involves, step by step

A correct installation is decided before any equipment is ordered. It begins with an ACCA Manual J load calculation of the building's actual heating and cooling load, followed by Manual S to select equipment matched to that load, and Manual D where ductwork is being designed or replaced. Efficiency is fixed by law at this stage too: since 1 January 2023 a split-system central air conditioner installed in the Department of Energy's Southeast region must meet at least 14.3 SEER2, and because the regional standard is enforced on the date of installation rather than the date of manufacture, a lower-rated unit cannot legally be fitted in Florida at all. Split-system heat pumps carry a national minimum of 14.3 SEER2 and 7.5 HSPF2.

On installation day the work follows a fixed order. Pull the mechanical permit that a Florida changeout requires. Recover the old refrigerant rather than venting it. Set the new outdoor unit and air handler, run or reuse the line set, purge with dry nitrogen while brazing so no oxide scale forms inside the tubing, pressure-test the joints with nitrogen, then evacuate the circuit below 500 microns and confirm it holds on a micron gauge before weighing in the charge. Commissioning closes the job out, with temperature split, static pressure, airflow, condensate drain slope and float switch all measured. A straight residential changeout is normally a single day.

The steps skipped at this stage are the ones that fail two summers later. Since 1 January 2025 new residential systems are built for lower-GWP refrigerants such as R-454B rather than R-410A under the EPA's AIM Act technology transition rules, which makes a clean, dry, properly evacuated circuit more important rather than less. Replacement generally beats repair once a Florida system passes the 10 to 15 year mark, once the compressor has failed, or once refrigerant is being lost repeatedly. Questions

Installation questions, answered

Common installation questions from Sarasota, Manatee and Charlotte county customers.

What size air conditioner does my home need?

The size must come from a Manual J load calculation, not from a square-footage rule of thumb. Manual J accounts for the house's orientation, window area and glazing, insulation levels, ceiling height, ductwork, air leakage and local design temperatures. Rules of thumb such as 'one ton per 500 square feet' routinely oversize Florida systems, and an oversized unit short-cycles, fails to dehumidify, and leaves the house cold and clammy while wearing the compressor out faster.

How long does an HVAC installation take?

A straight residential changeout — same system type, existing ductwork in good condition — is typically one day. Adding new ductwork, relocating an air handler, converting to a different system type, or installing in new construction takes two to three days. EagleAir confirms the schedule and the flat-rate price before the install date, so there is no open-ended labour clock.

Does a new system come with a warranty?

Yes. New equipment carries the manufacturer's parts warranty, and EagleAir warrants the installation labour. Registering the equipment with the manufacturer after installation is important — many brands extend the parts warranty substantially for registered systems, and the registration window is limited, usually to 60 or 90 days from installation.

Do I need a permit to replace an HVAC system in Florida?

Yes. A full system changeout is regulated mechanical work, and the local building department requires a permit and at least one inspection before the job is closed out; repairs to an existing unit generally do not. Every Florida county and municipality runs its own permitting process, and a licensed contractor normally pulls the permit on the homeowner's behalf, which is how EagleAir handles it. The permit matters beyond compliance: an unpermitted changeout can surface later during a home sale or an insurance claim, and it means no independent inspector ever confirmed the installation.

What efficiency rating am I required to buy in Florida?

At least 14.3 SEER2 for a split-system central air conditioner under 45,000 BTU, because Florida sits in the Department of Energy's Southeast region where that standard has applied since 1 January 2023. Larger split systems of 45,000 BTU and above are held to 13.8 SEER2, and split-system heat pumps to 14.3 SEER2 with 7.5 HSPF2 nationally. Since the Southeast standard is enforced at the point of installation, older non-compliant stock cannot legally be installed here even if it was manufactured earlier. Going above the minimum is worth modelling against your real runtime, because Florida's cooling season is long enough for efficiency differences to show up on the bill.
